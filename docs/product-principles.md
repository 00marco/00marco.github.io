# LivingNotes product principles

## Notes should rise and fall

> I want notes to rise and fall like civilizations. The goal is not strictly to prevent hoarding; it is to keep the garden from becoming stagnant and keep the contents of important notes in the user's head.
>
> — Marco, product direction

LivingNotes should treat notes as living structures. They can grow, mature, change, and eventually become less prominent. The app should encourage people to revisit, condense, rewrite, renew, or let go of old writing instead of allowing the garden to become a permanently expanding archive.

This is productive impermanence, not punishment. Mechanics that create change should be slow, predictable, and clearly communicated. Stored writing should remain safe and readable even when a note loses capacity or status. Revisiting a note should feel meaningful, not like repetitive maintenance.

Use this question when evaluating a feature:

> Does this keep the garden alive and bring useful ideas back into attention without making writing feel unsafe?

## What belongs in LivingNotes

LivingNotes is best for writing that benefits from being revisited, reshaped, and eventually released rather than stored unchanged forever.

Good fits include:

- Ideas in development
- Personal reflections and changing beliefs
- Active project thinking, decisions, and open questions
- Learning notes written in the user's own words
- Creative fragments such as premises, scenes, phrases, and themes
- Temporary priorities
- Notes for upcoming conversations
- Short-lived research and working conclusions
- Principles that should periodically prove they are still relevant
- Seasonal records and snapshots of a period of life

These notes have a natural lifecycle:

> Capture → revisit → expand → condense → integrate or let go

An old note does not necessarily fail when it declines. It can leave behind a smaller, denser successor: a principle, decision, memory, or seed for a new note.

LivingNotes is a poor fit for passwords, legal records, medical information, financial records, exact citations, permanent documentation, or anything whose precise wording must remain reliably available. Those belong in systems designed primarily for preservation.

The intended positioning is:

> LivingNotes is for thoughts you want to keep alive—not files you need to keep forever.
